The ten item cybersecurity checklist

Most small office breaches start with something on this list. None of it needs a big budget. It needs someone to sit down and check.

  1. Turn on MFA. Start with email, then remote access, then every admin account. Partial coverage does not count.
  2. List what you own. Computers, phones, servers, cloud accounts and who uses each one. You cannot protect what you forgot about.
  3. Back up and test. Keep one copy offline or separate from your network, and restore a file now and then to prove it works.
  4. Patch on a schedule. Turn on automatic updates. Replace anything the maker no longer supports.
  5. Use a password manager. No shared logins, no sticky notes, no reusing the same password.
  6. Protect every device. Run endpoint protection on each computer and turn on disk encryption on laptops.
  7. Train your staff. A short session on phishing a few times a year, with a sign in sheet to show it happened.
  8. Limit admin rights. Nobody should read email from an admin account.
  9. Write a one page incident plan. Who to call first, who to call second, and where your insurance policy number lives.
  10. Close the door when people leave. Shut off accounts and take back devices the same day.

Where Texas offices usually get stuck

Dental and medical offices, CPA and tax shops, law offices, insurance agencies and property managers all run into the same three walls.

  • The owner is the IT department, and security is the job that never reaches the top of the list.
  • The tools are bought but not switched on, or switched on for some people and not others.
  • Nothing is written down, so a client, a lender or an insurer asks for proof and there is none.

The checklist fixes the first two. Writing it down fixes the third. If you carry cyber insurance, expect the same items on the application. See how to get through a cyber insurance renewal questionnaire.

FAQ

Frequently asked questions

What should a small business cybersecurity checklist include?

At minimum: MFA on email and admin accounts, tested backups, patching, endpoint protection, staff training, limited admin rights, a written incident plan and a way to shut off accounts when people leave. The ten items above cover those.

Do small businesses in Texas need cybersecurity?

Yes. Attackers do not check office size. Small offices hold client records, bank logins and payroll, and often have less protection than a big company. Clients, lenders and insurers also ask for proof more often than they used to.

How long does it take to work through the checklist?

A small office can usually get through the first pass in an afternoon. Fixing the gaps takes longer, depending on how many items come back not yet true. Start with MFA and backups, since those matter most.
